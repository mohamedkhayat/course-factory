# Sequencing and structure: overview first, then detail

Researched 2026-11-11. Companion to learning-design.md (practice design); this file covers order and structure. Sources were
read from ERIC and Visible Learning pages, or from search snippets. Most journal pages returned 403 or a login redirect, so
each claim says how it was obtained. Numbers not confirmed from a page we opened are listed under "Citations to verify".

## Rules we could adopt

| Rule | Where it's checked | Evidence | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| A Section's first Block is prose that says what the Section covers and how it links to the previous Section | Block order | Luiten et al. 1980 (small effect); Stone 1983 (larger, contested); Mayer 1979 | weak |
| The Chapter plan lists Sections in order with headings; each Section has a heading and a one-line goal | Chapter plan, Block order | Lorch & Lorch 1995; signalling meta-analysis (d+ 0.32 to 0.39, snippet only) | moderate |
| A formal definition or theorem Block is preceded in its Section by a concrete example or contrasting cases | Block order | Schwartz & Bransford 1998; Fyfe et al. 2014 (direction only) | moderate |
| The Chapter's first Section is a simplified whole task (epitome); later Sections add complexity, not new topics | Chapter plan | Reigeluth simplifying conditions method; 4C/ID task classes | weak to moderate |
| At most 5 new formal terms per Section; each term is defined on first use | Block order, term count | Cowan 2001 (about 4 chunks); McNamara et al. 1996 | weak |
| A process of more than 5 steps is split into learner-advanced segments, preceded by a Block naming its components | Plan check, Block order | Rey et al. 2019 (segmenting); Cromley & Chen 2025 (pre-training g = 0.28, n.s.) | moderate (segmenting), weak (pre-training) |
| In a multi-element procedure, elements are introduced one at a time before the combined worked example | Plan check | Pollock, Chandler & Sweller 2002 (four small experiments) | weak |
| Novice Sections avoid unexplained references: connectives and term definitions are present | Block text (LLM check) | McNamara, Kintsch, Songer & Kintsch 1996 | moderate; reverses for high-knowledge readers |

## 1. Advance organizers

- Luiten, Ames & Ackerson (1980), American Educational Research Journal 17(2), 211-218, ERIC EJ233231 (https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ233231, opened). 135 studies. The abstract says organizers had "a small facilitative effect" on learning and retention. No number in the abstract. Other sources quote 0.21; that is unverified.
- Stone (1983), Journal of Experimental Education 51(4), 194-199, DOI 10.1080/00220973.1983.11011862 (publisher listing from search). 29 reports, 112 studies; the abstract says organizers were "associated with increased learning and retention". The d = 0.66 figure comes only from Visible Learning, not the paper.
- Visible Learning's aggregate (https://www.visiblelearningmetax.com/influences/view/advance_organizers, opened): weighted mean 0.42 over 901 studies and 10 meta-analyses. Its table total reads 0.46, which conflicts with the headline figure. This is a meta-meta-analysis and is not a primary source.
- Mayer (1979), Review of Educational Research 49(2), 371-383, DOI 10.3102/00346543049002371 (search only). Replies to Barnes & Clawson (1975), who concluded organizers "do not facilitate learning" as then built; Mayer argues effects appear when measured properly.

Implication: a short overview Block is cheap and its effect is small to moderate. The 0.21 vs 0.66 gap is mostly about study inclusion. Keep the overview to one paragraph; do not expect large gains from it alone.

## 2. Elaboration theory

Reigeluth and colleagues' simplifying conditions method (Reigeluth & Rodgers 1980) is described in a secondary summary of Reigeluth's work (AECT chapter 18.4, search snippet). Its two principles: an epitome (the simplest version still representative of the whole task) comes first, then progressively more complex elaborations. Van Patten, Chao & Reigeluth (1986), Review of Educational Research 56(4), DOI 10.3102/00346543056004437 (search only), reviews sequencing strategies. No effect sizes were retrieved.

Implication: the first Section of a Chapter should be a small, complete example of the whole topic, and later Sections deepen it. This is design theory, supported mainly by case work.

## 3. Pre-training, segmenting, isolated elements

- Pollock, Chandler & Sweller (2002), Learning and Instruction 12(1), 61-86 (ro.uow.edu.au record from search; page refused). Four experiments with 18 to 25 vocational students each. Isolated elements first, then the full material; the isolated-elements group did better on tests. Samples are small; no effect size retrieved.
- Rey, Beege, Nebel, Wirzberger, Schmitt & Schneider (2019), Educational Psychology Review 31, 389-419, DOI 10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4 (search). 56 investigations, 88 comparisons. The segmenting effect held for system-paced segmentation; a secondary source says it was not reliable when learners controlled the pace. Pooled d not retrieved.
- Cromley & Chen (2025), Educational Research Review 49, DOI not captured (ScienceDirect S1747938X25000673, refused; figures from search snippets). Overall g = 0.37 across Mayer's principles. Pre-training g = 0.28, CI -0.05 to 0.61, not significant.
- Sweller (2010), Educational Psychology Review 22, 123-138, DOI 10.1007/s10648-010-9128-5 (search). Intrinsic load is set by element interactivity.
- Cowan (2001), Behavioral and Brain Sciences 24(1), 87-114, DOI 10.1017/S0140525X01003922 (search). Working memory holds about four chunks.

Implication: segment long processes so the learner advances at their own pace; name components before a process diagram, but do not expect a large effect from pre-training alone; split high-interactivity procedures into isolated elements before the combined example.

## 4. 4C/ID

van Merriënboer & Kirschner, *Ten Steps to Complex Learning* (book; editions given as 2007 to 2024 across sources). Only secondary descriptions were read (4cid.org overview PDF in search results, not opened). The model uses whole tasks grouped into task classes of rising complexity, with support faded across classes. Part-task practice supplements whole-task practice. No effect-size meta-analysis found.

Implication: order Chapters as task classes, each a fuller version of the whole task. Strength moderate as a design framework.

## 5. Concreteness fading and telling

- Schwartz & Bransford (1998), Cognition and Instruction 16(4), 475-522 (some sources give 523). Three classroom experiments: students compared contrasting cases first, then heard a lecture or read a text, and predicted outcomes a week later. The PDF was unreadable through our tool; the claim comes from the ERIC record and summaries. No effect sizes found.
- Fyfe, McNeil, Son & Goldstone (2014), Educational Psychology Review 26, 9-25 (Springer, search). This is a systematic review of mathematics and science teaching, not a meta-analysis. The Springer page redirected to a login, so we did not read its conclusions.

Implication: a concrete case or contrast before the formal definition, then the definition, then the symbols. Evidence supports the order but not a size.

## 6. Signalling, headings and goals

- Lorch & Lorch (1995), Journal of Educational Psychology 87, 537-544 (search snippet). Organizational cues raised recall more than no cues.
- Lorch, Lorch & Inman (1993) (search snippet). Headings, overviews and summaries improved topic recall; about 11% more at immediate test and 44% more at delayed test. Not opened.
- Signalling meta-analysis, ScienceDirect S1747938X17300581 (403; figures from snippet). 103 studies, N = 12,201; d+ 0.32 to 0.39 for retention, comprehension and transfer. Authors and journal not captured.

Implication: headings and a one-line goal per Section are cheap and have a modest positive effect.

## 7. Text difficulty and coherence

McNamara, Kintsch, Songer & Kintsch (1996), Cognition and Instruction 14(1), 1-43, DOI 10.1207/s1532690xci1401_1 (search, abstract snippet). Low-knowledge readers learned more from a coherent text; high-knowledge readers learned more from a less coherent text, which made them infer. The reversal depended on conditions in later work. No primary number was found for terms per segment; the 5-term limit above is a heuristic.

Implication: novice Sections need explicit links (define before use, connect each paragraph to the last). Keep the density check weak until a source supports a number.

## 8. Section length and concepts per unit

No solid evidence found for a time or concept limit in self-paced online study. The term cap rests on Cowan (2001) and is a design heuristic.

## Conflicts

- Advance organizers: Luiten's small effect (about 0.21 per secondary) vs Stone's 0.66 vs Visible Learning's 0.42. Ausubel defended the model (1978 reply, title only from search).
- Pre-training: Cromley & Chen find no significant effect; older Mayer medians are larger.
- Coherence: helps novices, can hurt high-knowledge readers (McNamara et al.).
- Segmenting: works for system-paced, not reliably for learner-paced (secondary report).
- Concreteness fading: a systematic review supports the direction; no effect sizes retrieved.
